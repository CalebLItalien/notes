# The History of Software, Chapter 1
## Proposal
In this chapter, I will describe the origins of modern software in the United States, which occurred during World War II in the 1940s. This chapter will discuss the first usages of computers and the nature of organizations capable and willing to produce the capital required for these experimental projects. I hope to explain the rationale behind these undertakings and the reason low-wage female workers were exclusively used at first to configure these computers, along with a description of their conditions. I also want to explore the goals and makeup of organizations that existed at the time for computing, including the Association of Computing Machinery and the Institute of radio Engineers (which had a committee on electronic computers and would later form the IEEE). Finally, I want to explain how the foundation this period created would influence the later labor conditions of programming and capital investment in software projects.

The anticipation of World War 2 in the 1930s led to government funding for exploration into the science of automatic calculation devices. Seeing as these projects were experimental and considered extremely high risk, only the government and large owners of capital were wiling to provide the capital required for this undertaking. These inventions led to analog devices that were capable of  automatic calculation that were used in warfare and the underlying inventions required for later digital computers. While the technological advancements during this period are clear, it is less clear the types of workers that helped develop these projects and the conditions they were placed in. There is a lot of discussion in various histories of computing on the 'pioneers' of this period, but less so on the mechanical, repetitive work done and how these workers were selected, except that they were "female."

The problem of these early computers requiring massive amounts of capital to develop and build, along with the risk undertaken in even trying to develop these types of tools, laid a foundation  for the labor relations that would persist into the development of modern software in the 1940s and early 1950s. At the beginning of the decade, automatic computers were still theoretical, and  the human computation was still a job. Human computation was almost entirely dominated by women, since the work was mechanical and their pay was lower, as well as due to the gender politics of  the time. Due to this, when automatic computers were built and the configuring of them was still mechanical, women continued to be used and thus the ENIAC in its development was developed by women. However, when the device was moved to a military location, women would lose their jobs due to gender restrictions. It's not clear what type of workers would take their place.

The conditions of labor for these projects and the effects the products of this labor would have on industry are clearly not well known. However, it is known that large amounts of capital, backed by the federal government, was used on these high-risk projects. My goal is to explain the types of projects done, why they were done, how they were done, and what their legacy is today. My sources will include personal accounts and documents found through various archives and I hope through this research to illuminate the questions I have.
