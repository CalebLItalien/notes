## Chapter One: The Ancestors
### Pascal to Babbage: Getting into Gear
- 1642, Blaise Pascal develops an adding machine for a high official in France, new way of organizing taxation "in the face of revolt" (p. 13)
	- Used gears and hanging weights to achieve addition
- Gottrief Wilhelm Leibniz creates a machine capable of multiplication, division, subtraction, and addition based on Pascal's device
	- Used a drum instead of a wheel
	- Called the Stepped Reckoner
	- "Leibniz's calculation machine couldn't be a success because economics were against it, Vannevar Bush, one of the foremost American 
		scientists wrote in 1945. There were no savings in labor: Pencil and paper were still cheaper and faster."
- About the 1820s when mechanical calculators gained widespread use
	- Industrial revolution and mass-production
	- Charles Babbage envisions the computer at the Royal Astronomical Society "part of England's intellectual upper curst" (p. 14)
		- Was focusd on maritime navigation, astronomy, and insustry "doing calculations yourself required weeks and months of mindless
			number crunching" (p. 15)
- Babbage (never fully) builds the Difference Engine, a steam-powered device that produce mathematical tables
	- Began work in 1823, prototype in 1833
	- "Plagued by cost overruns and production difficulties ... exceeded the manufacturing capabilities of the nineteenth cnetury" (p. 15)
- 1833, Babbage has idea for a new machine, the Analytical Engine
	- "Similar in design to modern comptuers, the device could perform any kind of calculation, not just generate tables" (p. 16)
	- Used the Jacquard loom, which worked with punched cards
	- Had an assistant named Augusta Ada King
	- "By placing cards in an orderly series, Babbage could organize various cmoputational tasks - he could program his computer" (p. 17)
	- "It could hold partail results for further use. In effect, it could store information" (p. 17)
	- Was capable of conditional statements
	- British government refused to fund it

### The Dawn of "Data Processing"
- ""Data Processing" became a mechanical function in 1890 when Herman Hollerith of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology built a 
punch-card tabulator for the U.S. Census Bureau" (p. 18)
	- "1880 bureau had taken  nearly 7 years to count ... 1890 census might take ten years" (p. 18)
	- Hollerith used punch cards that stored information
	- Machine was capable of sorting punch cards based on different organizational schemes
	- "Initial results from the 1890 census were tabulated in just six weeks"
	- Census bureau declared new population of 62,622,250, pundits had hoped for 75 million, and machine was blamed "Useless Machines" 
		by Boston Herald and "Ship Shod Work Has Spoiled the Census" by New York Herald
- 1890 census was completed in less than 3 years with $5 million savings and higher accuracy
- Hollerith's ideas used at racetracks for bets and odds
- 1896, Hollerith's ideas become the Tabulating Machine Company, eventually to become IBM
- 1935, Social Security Administration comes into existence "using punch cards to store wage data on every working American" (p. 20)
- Scientists wanted faster machines with more capabilities
	- MIT at heart of research
- 1930, MIT's Vannevar Bush makes the Differential Analzyer
	- "measured movements and distances to yield the results of computations based on those measurements" (p. 21)
	- "Programming it was a major chore, since all the interconections had to be undone and redone" (p. 21)
	- Could only solve differential equations
	- Was analog rather than digital, "worked in continous waves rather than specific digits ... analog devices are less accurate because
		the readout is an estimate" (p. 22)
	- Late the groundwork for proving electricity the fastest way to do calculations

### Electrical Calculators Make Their Mark
- "Bell Telephone Laboratories had been working for years on the science of turning information ... into electrical signals." (p. 23)
	- "Early switches required operators to physically connect lines with patch cords ... relays were developed to make the connections much
		faster" (p. 23)
- Mid-1930s, George R. Stibitz at Bell Labs used relays to represent 1 or 0, with a circuit of relays called a flip-flop, could count in 
	binary arithmetic
	- "If you could sling enough relays together, you could make a very fast calculator. Instead of numbers being represented by slow-moving
		wheels and drums, they could be represented by fast-moving electricity."
	- 1937, Stibitz makes plan for a machine to handle computations for voice circuitry, the Complex Number Computer
		- "Couldn't be programmed; once numbers were fed in, it multiplied them automatically. It couldn't do further calculation with the
			result on its own" (p. 24)
- 1937, "Howard Aiken at Harvard was working on cmoputing machines when he stumbled upon Babbage's work. He began working on machines with IBM 
	that would become electromagnetic versions of the Hollerith punch cards" (p. 25)
	- "IBM, funding Aiken's work" (p. 25)
	- IBM was first Hollerith's Tabulating Machine Company, then sold to Charles Ranleigh, who merged with shopkeeper's scales and workplace time
		clocks companies to create Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company (CTR), and in 1924 renamed to IBM
	- Thomas J. Watson Sr. - "Everywhere ... there will be IBM machines in use. The sun never sets on IBM" (p. 25)
	- IBM had development laboratories and funded unviersity research at several institutions
- Howard Aiken builds the Mark I, an automatic computing machine "a slow electromechanical giant" (p. 25) inspired by Babbage's work
	- 5 tons
	- "First fully automatic machine: It could run for hours or even days without operator intervention" (p. 26)
	- No conditional statements and very slow
	- Became part of the computations for the U.S. Navy
	- 1944, IBM begins clashing with Aiken over the display vs. practicality of the device in its unveiling
	- According to Martin Campbell-Kelly and William Aspray, "Aiken took credit as the sole inventor of the calculator" (p. 27)
		- Didn't acknowledge IBM's undewriting or contribution of engineers
	- "Had not made the leap to using electricity as the counting and calculating component itself"

### Pipe Dreams in the Lab

## Chapter Three: Crunched by Numbers
- 1943, Lieutenant Herman Goldstine pitched project to U.S. Army (expensive, TS)
- Goldtine had been assigned to the Ballistics Research Laboratory in the draft
	- At Aberdeen (location of above) military tested artillery and other weapons, artillery required extensive firing tables
	- Calculations for firing tables were done at Aberdeen
	- "It took more than a month to produce a complete firing table ... Aberdeen had a team of women doing the calculating, using desk 
		calculators with push buttons and a large handle to pull to copmlete the arithmetic operation [human computers]." (p. 54)
- Early 1943, Allies losing the war, "Aberdeen was falling far behind in its firing table responsibility, and guns were being delivered to
	Europe and Africa that were essentially useless because they could not be aimed. ... Tables made for Europe, the army found, did not
	work in Africa ... because the ground was softer" (p. 54)
- 1942, Army contracts with Pen's Moore School because the school had a Differential Analyzer, as well as a civil-service human computing team
	- Was not enough to meet demand
		- Goldstine sent his wife to look for more female math majors at colleges, but only a handful
	- Goldstine was in charge of the operation at Penn
	- Ran the Differential Analyzer as much as possible, but was prone to breakdowns

### An Ingenious Proposal
- Goldstine approaches Mauchly about his ideas of an electronic computer
	- Mauchly goes to Differential Analyzer to see firing-table workers, there exist a recollection of him by one of the women "computers"
- Mauchly has a diary
- Goldstine, Mauchly, and Brainert travle to "top brass" in Aberdeen
- "At that time during the war, people were looking for anybody with new ideas" - Lila Butler (p. 58), a human computer
- Officers at Aberdeen had already denied proposal of nuclear fission bomb, only to be overruled by FDR

### "Simon, Give Goldstine the Money"
- "Meeting included Colonel Leslie E. Simon, director of the Ballistics Research Laboratory, and Oswald Veblen, a renowned mathematician and 
	technical adviser to the army laboratory" (p. 59-60)
- "[The electronic computer] was pitched not as a new science but rather as a conversion of existing technology to electronics. They proposed
	a machine that would, in essence, string together a dozen desk calculators and allow them to ... [use] the results of one calculation 
	in another to solve complex problems. It was to be a flexible, general-purpose machine, capable of not only crunching firing tables
	but also performing wind tunnel calculations and maybe even weather-pattern predictions." (p. 60)
- They decide to fund the project, dubbed Project PX
	- Army gives University of Pennsylvania development contract W-670-ORD-4926 with $61,700 for 6 months of work on what was originaly called the Electronic Numerical Integrator, later changed to Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer

## Chapter Four: Getting Started
- Unlike Aiken, who had support of IBM engineers, ENIAC project just had the researchers
- In the planning of how the device would work "there would be no paper tape, because anything on paper was too slow. Punch cards would be used only in the initial phase of inputting the original problem." (p. 63)
	- Required a "master programmer" to control the device ("memory" and "programmer" were already popularized computer terms) (p. 63)

### Assembling the ENIAC Team
- Project began in 1943 with 12 people in the Moore School building
	- No senior faculty however
	- Bob Shaw (engineer) worked on the project
- "Allied troops had invaded Sicily in July 1943, just as the ENIAC project started, and the U.S. Air Force was bombing Rome in advance of an invasion. The need for firing tables was at a fever pitch, and the ENIAC project operated every hour of the day and night." (p. 66-67)

### The First Computer Takes Shape
- RCA (Radio Corporation of America) agrees to advise on the project but not to be a subcontractor
- ENIAC was base 10 to be more familiar
- "Opening up the accumulators while ENIAC was powered up was so dangerous, Eckert designed safety switches into the door panels that would shut the voltage down if opened, so no one would be electrocuted." (p. 74)
- "Many eminent scientists of the day, including those at MIT, doubted ENIAC could ever run accurately because it relied on so many unreliable vacuum tubes." (p. 75)
	- "The biggest use of vacuum tubes at the time was a couple hundred in a counter built at Los Alamos for the Manhattan Prject." (p. 76)
	- "The problem was even worse because tub quality was declining as the war drained skilled labor from factories." (p. 76)
- Eckert was so paranoid about ENIAC breaking down that he starved mice and then figured out which types of wire they found the least appetizing to use in ENIAC
- Telephone company workers brought on to the project to do wiring inside the machine, "few cmopanies were opening new offices" (p. 77) so they were available
- Bell Labs helped with the parts of ENIAC involving telphone parts, IBM made a special card-reading machine for it, engineering firm designs ventilation system
- "The master programmer would make the machine automatic, and very fast. And it would make it fundamentally different from a calculator: The computer can do something with the numbers it produces." (p. 78)
- Mauchly allegedly unaware of Babbage's idea of a subroutine (if ... then) and comes up with it on his own
- Army scientific leadership in Alberdeen pressures ENIAC team to expand scope of what project would be capable of
- Moore School given the nickname Whistle Factory
- "The pace grew even more relentless. The engineers became the prototypes for the original Silicon Valley nerds. Often they worked around the cock" (p. 79)
	- See Herman Lukoff for personal account

### The Team Leaders: Contrasting Styles and Personalities

### "A Son-of-a-Bitch to Program"
- "One person could run the machine, and the average running time before something went wrong was between five and six hours. Preparing a program for the machine took a month or two, and setting up the machine took a day or two" (p. 94)
	- "The Eniac was a son-of-a-bitch to program" - Jean Bartik "one of the original programmers" (p. 94)
- "Programming itself appears to have been largely an afterthought." (p. 94-95)

### The Women Who Programmed ENIAC
- "Six women were chosen from among the several hundred human comptuers to work on ENIAC, making them the world's first computer programmers: Frances Bilas Spence, Elizabeth Jennings, Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum, Kathleen McNulty, Elizabeth Snyder Holberton, and Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer." (p. 95)
	- They had prevoiusly worked on balistic firing tables at Philadelphia and worked on the differential Analyzer
	- They were senht to Aberdeen to learn IBM tabulating equipment
- "Even though the women [programmers] had significant responsibility for the success of the prjoect, they continued to be treated as clers, although they were in fact programmers." (p. 97)

### Brainerd Fights for Ownership of the Project

### ENIAC's Belated - but Impressive - Debut
- War was far from over in fall of 1944
- Fall of 1945, ENIAC was ready to be used, "hardware was wroking [and] the programmers had learned how to run it." (p. 101)
	- Took 200,000 man-hours of work and $486,804.22
	- "Too late for the purpose for which it was built" (p. 101)
- "It was 1,000 times faster than any numerical calculator, 500 times faster than any existing computing machine. It could perform 5000 addition cycles a second and do the work of 50,000 people working by hand." (p. 101)
- Required 174 kilowatts of power to run and cost $650 per hour in electricty when not operating
- "With the war over and the need for firing tables evaporating, ... the army decided ENIAC could be of use on ... nuclear weapons." 
	- Nicholas Metropolis and Stanely Frankel solved a problem on ENIAC about hydrogen bombs that remains classified
		- "ENIAC correctly showed that Teller's scheme would not work, but the results led Teller and Ulam to come up with another design together." (p. 103)
- "Early 1946, the army and the University of Pennsylvania were ready to unveil their creation to the public." (p. 104)
	- Were roing to run a ballistic trajectory on ENIAC, but had a bug. "The women worked day and night trying to pinpoint the problem" (p. 105)
- "A first step ... in applying electronics to the numerical work of science and engineering" - War Department Press Release
- "The group" - "It is inevitable that future machines willb e improved ... they will find application in all diverse fields where the tremendous volume of computations required seriously impede progress." (p. 107)
- Mauchly believed that the computer would reduce the price of bread or taxes
- "The War Department tonight unveiled ... the mathematical way to beter living for every man." - Associated Press (p. 107)
- Russians instantly try to order an ENIAC from University of Pennsylvania and are denied
- ENIAC tkaen apart and brought to Aberdeen to work on hydren bomb problems, Russian weather patterns, wind tunnel design, and ballistic tables
- "We did a lot of calculations we never knew what they were for" - Jospeh Chernow, chief engineer on ENIAC at Aberdeen from 1951 to 1953

### The First Draft: A Critical Document
- June 1945, Goldstine compiles von Neumann's notes and writes "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, by John von Neumann"
- 1947 memo by Warren states that Goldstine believed the inormation to not reaquire classification (classified)
- "Von Neumann credited only one suggestion to Mauchly and made no mention of anyone else on the project." (p. 119)
	- Eckert's memos frmo 1944 had laid down a lot of its ideas, "the device he envisioned would replace much of the difficult mechanical programming the women were doing on ENIAC." (p. 119-120)
- Eckert and Muahcly realize they were losing control over their own ideas since Goldstine spread the report to academics, and 3 months after the inital report and before contract had even been signed they wrote "Progress Report on EDVAC"
	- This report was made confidential by Goldstine
- "Distribution of the First Draft document resulted in exactly the kind of frenzy that von Neumann and Goldstine had apparently wanted." (p. 122)
	- Academics thought this dissemination was best to stop it being monopolized by commercial interests
	- Universities across the globe start building them based on the document
	- Von Neumann's further lectures made it "easier for institutions to raise research money" (p. 123)
	- "[Von Neumann] appreciated at once the possibilities of what ecame known as logical design and the potentialities implicit in the stored program principle." (p. 124)
- A month before the unveiling of ENIAC, story in New York times (1946) on von Neumann and RCA's Dr. Zworykin building their own machine for the U.S. Weather Bureau
- 1980, article in the Annals of the History of Computing, Nicholas Metroplis reported that the stored-program concept predated von Neumann's participation on EDVAC

### Battles over Patents
- As early as August 1944 Moore School had been working on patents for ENIAC
	- The government would get to build, use, or sell computers for any purpose without fee
	- Penn and other academic institutions would get a license to build computers for noncommercial purposes
- 1946, Gillon writes to Moore School that classification would soon be lifed on ENIAC
- Goldstine, the Moore School, Eckert/Mauchly all start battling for patent over ENIAC
- Irven Travis (Penn Professor) states that all employees of the university must turn over patents to the university
	- "Several of teh faculty at the Moore School conducted research at the university and had companies on the side to market products resulting from research." (p. 131)
- Eckert writes to Colonel Gillon on 1946 asking for them to pressure Penn into giving Eckert/Mauchly the same patent deal they had gotten on ENIAC
- Dean Pender sends an ultimatum over the patents issue and Eckert/Mauchly resign (though they'd say they were fired by Travis, who agreed)
- Maurice Wilkes' EDSAC machine would end up being the first computer with the stored-program design
- Penn gave up its lead on MIT and Harvard and stopped computer development after EDVAC
- "They [Penn administrators] asked too late. They blew the whole thing ... When they didn't have the war to hold it together, it all fell apart." - Goldstine (p. 133)

## Chapter Seven: Out on Their Own
- "From the start, both Eckert and Mauchly intuitively recognized that the comptuer had enormous commercial applciation, and they both thought its development would be far faster in the business world than in academe." (p. 135)
	- Understood this in 1945
	- Von Neumann saw it as a new field
	- Moore School administrators saw it this way
- Mauchly had been visiting with the U.S. Census Bureau and the U.S. Weather Bureau on their interest in computing machines
- Eckert's father had his own commercial success and knew people with money in Philadelphia who could invest in Eckert/Mauchly's potential new company
- Von Neumann invited Eckert to become a chief engineer at Princeton's Institute of Advanced Study
	- Eckert declined and saw this as "shortsighted, greedy inclinations, obviously fostered by his capitalist father." (p. 137)
- "We knew the only way this was going to fly was to get a lot of commercial money behind it." - Eckert in 1980 interview
- Thomas Watson Sr. of IBM (they'd provided the punch-card machines) offered Eckert and Mauchly computer labs in 1946
	- Mauchly thoguth IBM price-gouged people for punch-card machines

### Launching a New Industry
- Summer of 1946 the Pentagon through the Navy's Office of Naval Research and the Ordnance Department puts on a class on Digital Computers, helped taught by Eckert and Mauchly "extraordinary effort to jump-start research in the field" (p. 140)
	- Became known as The Moore School Lectures
	- One of the main issues was digital over analog, Harvard and MIT sstill not convinced
- Office of Naval Research funded MIT and Harvard for computers
- Natinoal Bureau of Standards on behalf of the U.S. Census Brueau vetted Eckert-Mauchly's proposal using George Stibitz of Bell Labs
	- Ended up going against Stibitz' advice (who liked analog) and funding the project, $270,000 over two years for EDVAC II
- Right after getting the contract Mauchly's wife drowns
	- Even so, the contract with the Bureau of Standards continued, raised some money from friends and got Eckert's father to give a loan of $25k
- Eckert/Mauchly create the Electronic Control Company
	- "The original business plan talked of devices for scientific laboratories, universities, and research foundations; industrial research and engineering; government agencies; accounting and bookkeeping of large business firms; inventory, stock control, and planning departments of large cmopanies, insurance agencies, and other institutions with volumnious files" (p. 144)
	- Thought their devices could be used for instant transactions for banks, department stores, trains, etc.
		- Business plan written and sent to brokers in 1946
	- Curtiss at Bureau of Standards realized ECC had limited capital so had other governemtn agencies contract with them, Air Controller's Office and Army Map Service did

### Patent Issues Pending

### The First Year in Business
- August, Eckert/Mauchly start work on UNIVAC (Universal Automatic Computer)
- "They worked long hours, six and seven days a week, just as they had on ENIAC" (p. 148)
	- "hired their engineering staff from Penn ... and other institutions ... Eckert paid close attention in interviews [to] hobbies; people with technical hobbies often were hired, because he figured they would better tolerate working on technical projects around the clock without breaks." (p. 149)
		- Earl Masterston was interviewed by Eckert and Frzier Welsh on a Saturday, 7 hour interview
	- "Eckert came in late, at about 11 A.M., and worked well into the night." (p. 150)
- "He [Mauchly] and Elizabeth Snyder Hoberton, one of the programmers who, like Jean Bartik, had joined the company, worked on various ways to give computers instructions." (p. 151)

- "Mauchly's side was a democracy; the engineering side was a dictatorship" (p. 153)
- "He [Mauchly] was often called upon to hustle up new investors, to meet the growing need for capital." (p. 153)
- "October 1947, Electronic Control Company was so starved for cash it signed a contract for another type of computer, even thoug the UNIVAC project was still in its infancy ... agreed to build a computer for Northrop that would control the flight of a new missiles called the SNARK." (p. 153)

## Chapter 8: Whose Idea Was It, Anyway?
- "IBM didn't plunge into computing because it foresaw the future of the computer ... this was a defensive action ... to protect IBM's dominance of office machinery should these new comptuers catch on." (p. 175)
- 1924, Thomas Watson Sr. took over IBM (grown out of Herman Hollerith's punch-card business) "on a crusade to dominate every line of office machine." (p. 175)
	- Recruits Wallce Eckert (no relation to Pres Eckert) and established Watson Computer Laboratory at Columbia
	- "My god, here we are trying to build Defense Calculators, while UNIVAC is smart enough to start taking all the civilian business away!' I was terrified" - Watson Jr. in his autobiography (p. 176)
- The Model 650 was not as good as UNIVAC, but it was compatiable with existing punch-card equipmetn
	- "Even fit into the same space as punch-card readers" (p. 176)
- 1952, "U.S. Justice Department had filed an antitrust suit against IBM for its control of the office machines market." (p. 176)
	- 1956 IBM forced to sell machines, not just lease, and divset some punch-card operations
	- Had to sell competitors a license to build compatible machines
		- "If cmopetitors couldn't sell products that would work with IBM office machiens, they would likely be shut out completely from many offices." (p. 177)'

### Sperry Takes on IBM and the Other Six Dwarfs
- IBM and Remington Rand (which would become Sperry before settling this deal) agree to "cross-licensing deal under which IBM agreed to pay $10 million over an eight-year period, plus a $1.1 million down payment, for computer royalties - once Sperry received the ENIAC patent." (p. 177)
- Both IBM and Bell Labs had challenged the ENIAC patent, IBM going as far as tracking down Atanasoff to see if Mauchly had gotten ideas from him to try to invalidate the patent
- 1962, District Judge Archie dawson upholds patent against American Telephone and Telegraph (Bell Labs)
- 1967, Honeywell and Control Data Corporation both sue Sperry, Sperry at least sues Honeywell back over new ENIAC patent and the royalties discussion reaching an impasse
	- Cross-licensing deal with IBM was no longer secret and IBM was paying less than Honeywell was getting demanded for, despite being 16 times smaller
	- With the help of Iowa State and the R.K. Richards book on comptuer development, tracked down Atanasoff
- John Atanasoff worked on his computer at Iowa State with Clifford Berry
	- 1930s, IBM rejected it for funding, but consulted with MIT and Bell Labs
	- Was in the Des Moines Tribune on Jan. 15, 1941, under the headline "Machine Remembers"
	- Abandoned the project during WW2 and worked at Naval Ordnance Laboratory
	- Was "a special-purpose machine capable of solving only one kind of problem" (p. 181)
	- Was not programmable and very different from ENIAC, essentially had no similarities
	- "That [that interim results had to be reentered for usage] was the fault of Atanasoff's machine ... could only run at human speed." - Mauchly (p. 182-183)
	- Never really worked

### Mauchly and Atanasoff Square Off in the Court
- 1971, trial opens, went for 135 days and the transcript was 20,667 pages
- Atanasoff had called the machine the Atanasoff Computing Mahcine, he and Honeywell's lawyers called it the Atanasoff-Berry Computer (ABC)
- "[Atanasoff] beliefed the overall concept of an automatic electronic digital comptuer was derived from ABC" even though there were no specific parts that were a copy (p. 186)
- Mauchly demonstrated with letters that he was interested in the notion of a digital comptuer before visiting Atanasoff
- Mauchly also lied on the stand which didn't help

### The Judge Hands Down His Decision
- Judge decides since the Los Alamos experiment ran in 1945 on the machine, and the patent application was filed in 1947, and a person has to file for a patent within 6 months of its first public use, the patent was invalid
	- Also was unveiled a year before patent applicatoin and was in Nature magazine article on April 20, 1946
	- First Draft report was also relevant

### Did the Judge Do the Right Thing?
- "IBM commanded what appeared to be exclusive use of the patent on computers - the ENIAC patent - through the once-secret cross-licensing deal it had made with Sperry in 1956." (p. 199)
- "In his ruling, the judge said the 1956 deal, details of which he found the two companies had tried to keep secret, was stifling the induustry, noting that in no other industry was technological development so important." (p. 199)
	- At the time it was referred to as the Electronic Data Processing (EDP) industry
- "The adolescent computer industry was beginning to have an enormous impact on companies and government, and its development was vital to the U.S. economy." (p. 200)
- ENIAC patent is invalidated
